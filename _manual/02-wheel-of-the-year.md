The year on the ridge turns through **eight points**. Four are set by the sun: the two solstices and the two equinoxes. Four fall roughly between them and are set by the work of the land. The names are northern ones; other traditions keep the same eight under other names.

## The eight turnings

| Turning | Around | The sky | What the homestead does |
|---|---|---|---|
| Yule | 21 Dec | longest night | rest, repair, take stock of the larder |
| Disting | early Feb | light returning | seed orders, tool sharpening, first plans |
| Ostara | 20 Mar | day equals night | seed starting indoors, soil waking |
| Walpurgis | 30 Apr | warming nights | beds prepared, flock out on fresh ground |
| Midsummer | 21 Jun | longest day | growth, watering, feeding the soil |
| Freyfaxi | 1 Aug | first harvest | first cutting, preserving, giving thanks |
| Winter Finding | 22 Sep | night equals day | harvest in, accounts squared, winter begins |
| Winter Nights | mid-Oct | dark gaining | honour the ancestors, finish the harvest, close up |

The [Wheel of the Year]({{ '/wheel-of-the-year/' | relative_url }}) page draws these as a circle with a hand that points to today, and gathers every dispatch under its season.

## Why the sun sets four of the dates

The solstices and equinoxes are not tradition; they are geometry. At this latitude, a little under 42 degrees north, the day runs from about 9 hours at Yule to about 15 hours at Midsummer. The equinoxes are the two points where the gain or loss is fastest: in late September the ridge loses about two and a half minutes of daylight a day. You feel that in the garden before you see it on a calendar.

## Why the land sets the other four

The cross-quarter days follow the work, and the work follows the weather, so the lodge keeps them loosely:

- **Disting** is when the light is clearly back but nothing grows. It is for planning.
- **Walpurgis** is when frost risk drops enough to trust the beds.
- **Freyfaxi** is the first real harvest, whatever the date says.
- **Winter Nights** comes with the first hard frosts. The ancestors are honoured when the harvest is in.

## Keeping a turning

A turning does not need a grand rite. The lodge's habit is:

1. **Measure it.** Sunrise, sunset, day length, the moon, the first frost or the last. The measure boxes at the top of the dispatches come from this.
2. **Name what changed.** In the garden, the flock, the house, the work.
3. **Close what belongs to the season ending.** Harvest in, accounts squared, the stale task closed and marked as stale.
4. **Give thanks and make an offering:** a cup poured out for the land-wights, a candle, a meal shared.
5. **Write it down.** The dispatches are the lodge's record of the wheel actually turning, year over year.

## The moon inside the wheel

The moon keeps a faster rhythm inside the year's: about 29.5 days from new to new. The lodge notes its phase in each measure box and on the home page, worked out from a known new moon and the length of the month. Full moons get their old names (the Harvest Moon is the full moon nearest Winter Finding), and a waning moon is a good season for clearing out.

The runes on this site are the **Elder Futhark**: twenty-four staves used across the Germanic world from roughly the second to the eighth century. They were letters first. Each stave stood for a sound, and each had a name that was also a word: *fehu*, cattle; *isa*, ice; *dagaz*, day. The names are why the runes carry meaning, and why they still reward attention.

## Where the meanings come from

Almost nothing survives of how the old north used runes for divination. What survives is better than guesswork, and worth knowing:

- **The rune poems.** Three medieval poems, one Old English, one Old Norwegian and one Old Icelandic, give a short verse for each stave's name. They are the oldest real commentary on what each name meant to the people who used it.
- **The names themselves.** A stave called *hail* means hail. Start with the plain word before any gloss.
- **Inscriptions.** Memorial stones, amulets and carved objects show the runes in use, mostly as writing, sometimes as charms.
- **Long practice.** Much of what modern rune books say comes from the last hundred years. That does not make it worthless, but it should be called what it is.

The glosses in the [Rune Index]({{ '/runes/' | relative_url }}) lean on the first two and say so. When a dispatch draws a lesson from a stave, it is one reading, not the reading.

## The three aettir

The futhark is grouped into three families of eight, the **aettir**:

| Aett | Staves | The old association |
|---|---|---|
| Freyr's | Fehu to Wunjo | wealth, strength, speech, the road, the gift, joy |
| Hagal's | Hagalaz to Sowilo | hail, need, ice, harvest, the yew, the lot-cup, warding, the sun |
| Tyr's | Tiwaz to Othala | justice, birch, horse, humankind, water, the seed, day, the home ground |

Read an aett as a story and it hangs together: the first is the world of goods and people, the second the forces you cannot command, the third the bonds that hold a life and a house.

## Pulling a rune for the day

The lodge's practice is simple and repeatable:

1. **Ask one plain question.** "What does today ask of me?" is enough.
2. **Draw one stave.** From a bag, or by any honest chance. The home page's rune of the day is drawn by the calendar, so every reader that day gets the same stave.
3. **Start with the name.** Ice is ice. Ask what in the day is frozen, held, waiting.
4. **Read the poem's verse,** then the gloss.
5. **Write down what you saw** and check it against the evening. The writing is the point: over months it shows which readings held and which were wishful.

Two staves for one day is a common pattern in the dispatches: one for the work, one for the lesson. Raidho and Isa, for instance: go in good order, and let the unfinished thing wait.

## Keeping it honest

- **A reading is a lens, not a forecast.** It helps you notice. It does not replace checking the weather, the gauge or the ledger.
- **Say where a meaning came from.** "The rune poem says" and "modern practice says" are both fine. Mixing them up is not.
- **Do not borrow sacred things carelessly.** The runes belong to a living tradition and to the dead who carved them. Treat them as you would a guest's heirloom.

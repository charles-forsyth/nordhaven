## The First Day of the Dark Half

Yesterday the scales stood level. Today they tipped.

It is a little after eight in the evening on the ridge. The moon is waxing gibbous, about nine-tenths lit, a few nights short of the Harvest Moon. The dark has the upper hand now, and it will keep it until spring.

Winter Finding is not only a feast. It is an inventory. The old year's work gets counted, what will not survive the cold gets culled, and the door gets barred against what does not belong. Looking back over this one ordinary Wednesday, that is almost exactly what the day turned out to be.

---

## A Retrospective in Four Movements

### 1. The Hearth Comes First

The day opened the old way: the flock let out into the morning, coffee poured, an aching back tended to, and the plan for the day laid out slowly instead of rushed.

There is a reason the old households started with the animals and the fire. Everything else that happens in a day rests on those two things being done. A day that begins at the hearth begins in the right order.

### 2. Kin at the Door

Mid-morning, a young woman of the family came by, upset. She had an important interview the next day and nothing she felt good wearing, and no money for anything new.

So the day waited. She was fed, and she was heard, and nobody hurried her out the door to keep a schedule. The errand could come later.

This is Frigg's kind of work, and it never makes it into anyone's logbook. It is not measured, it produces nothing you can point to, and it is the most important thing done all day. A household that cannot pause for its own is not really a household.

### 3. The Traveling House Comes Home

In the afternoon the camper came back from the repair shop across the state line, finished and without charge, and was set down, leveled, and unhooked on home ground.

A traveler's house returned whole at the turn of the season is a good omen by any reckoning. It will sit through the dark half, and when the light comes back it will carry us somewhere. The land-wights of this ridge have one more thing in their keeping tonight.

### 4. Counting True and Barring the Door

The rest of the day went to the quiet work that nobody sees: keeping honest measure on the systems that run the house and the work.

- **A report that had been lying.** A morning summary had been quietly printing the same invented number every day in place of a real one. It looked fine. It was fiction. It now reports what the sensors actually measure.
- **Buttons that always said yes.** Several controls announced "success" whether or not the thing they started actually worked. They now check, and they say so when it failed.
- **Two ledgers, counted against each other.** One set of books said what the limits were; another said what had actually been spent. They were pulled independently, compared line by line, and they agreed. Counting twice is how you earn the right to trust the count.
- **The threshold cleared.** On a remote machine, nine old accounts that no one used anymore were retired, keys belonging to computers long since gone were removed, and the door that faced the open road was shut. Only the people who belong there can come in now. Everything removed was set aside first, not burned, in case it is ever needed again.

That last one is the autumn cull in a different form. It is the same instinct that walks the fence line before the snow, and decides which of the flock will winter over. Heimdall stands at the bridge and knows who belongs. A well-kept threshold does the same.

## Troth, Again

Two nights ago this blog said that a system which lies about its own health is worse than no system at all. Today was that idea put to work.

The lesson from the lying report is worth writing down plainly: **the most dangerous failure is the one that produces plausible output.** A crash announces itself. A fallback value that looks like real data does not. It sits there, day after day, spending your trust and returning nothing, until the day you make a decision on it.

The fix is not cleverness. It is troth:

1. When you do not have the real number, **say you do not have it.**
2. When an action might have failed, **check before you claim it worked.**
3. When two sources should agree, **count both and compare.**
4. When you remove something, **keep it somewhere safe first.**

Tyr put his hand in the wolf's mouth as surety for an oath he knew would be broken. He paid for the truth with his own flesh. Nothing asked of us today cost anything like that. Saying "I don't know" costs a little pride at most.

---

## What Still Sits on the Scale

Not everything was settled. Some larger questions about who holds the keys to what were looked at, understood, and deliberately left for another day, when there is time to do them properly and check the work.

That is also part of the dark half's wisdom. Not every door must be barred by nightfall. Some work is better carried into the cold season and done carefully than rushed in the last light and done wrong.

**Hail Tyr**, keeper of the true measure, who asks only that we say what is so.

May everyone under this roof be warm and whole.
May the flock roost safe and the fences hold.
May the young one walk into her interview standing tall. May they see her, and not her clothes, and may the right door open.
May the traveling house carry good journeys and always come home.
May what was measured stay true, what was shut stay shut, and what was left for tomorrow wait patiently.

*A day told from the hearth after the last merge. Most of it was machines again, and some of it was the steward himself. This is what it meant, and what tomorrow asks.*

## The Hall at Nine

The hall is quiet now, and cold enough that the stove is worth feeding. The morning low touched thirty-four, one degree above the line where the coop alarm would have woken someone, and the day climbed only to the low fifties under a sky with no cloud in it at all. Tomorrow is the gentler one: mid-sixties, dry, a little wind.

The moon is a waning crescent tonight, about a fifth lit, and it will not rise until the small hours. Whoever is up at four will see it. Nobody else will.

Between the first question this morning and this sentence, the steward put one hundred and sixteen commits into the ground across eight corners of the estate and merged nineteen gates. One tool went from a plan on paper to a public release. One ledger was made harder to lie to. Eight signposts went up for strangers. And for the first time in a long while, the steward spent half a day not on the machines but on how he himself is seen.

## Chapter 1: Raidho, and a Road to the Far Cluster

Far to the west there is a great shared cluster. It does not belong to any one school. It is held in common by many of them, and anyone at a member school may ask to work there. The machines are generous and free to use. The catch has always been the gate: to put work on it, a researcher must learn a whole language of containers and manifests and quotas, and most of them have papers to write instead.

This morning that gate had a plan on paper. By ten to eleven it had a working tool with eight parts. By noon it had nine.

The tool sits between a researcher and the helper they already talk to. They say what they want in plain words: *run this script on a graphics card*, *run it two hundred times with different seeds*, *move my old batch job over*, *put my lab's little web tool on the open web*. The tool reads their project, writes the instructions the far cluster needs, checks every one of them against the far cluster's own rules, explains the plan, and runs nothing until the person says yes.

It was tested on the real thing all morning. A sweep of tasks ran and finished. A notebook opened. A small web tool for measuring leaves went live on the open web with a proper secure address in about a minute, and then, at the steward's word, it came down again and was cleaned away. When the helper put that tool up, the steward stopped short and asked what exactly had just been raised on someone else's cluster. It was a fair question to ask. A man who hands out keys ought to know which doors his own hand is opening.

Then came the part Raidho cares about most, which is the order of the going. Before a road is given to strangers, it has to start where they actually stand. So the tool learned to look at a person's own laptop first: is the cluster's command-line program there, is it the right version, is the sign-in piece installed, is the personal key file in place. Where something is missing, it offers to fix it, and asks first.

By a quarter to one the repository was public. By a quarter past one it had been proven to set itself up on all three of the common operating systems, by machines that had never seen it before. By five it had been through four more releases, the last of them fixing a cap that had counted a whole sweep against the limit instead of only what runs at once.

**Raidho** is the rune of the ride and of right order. It is not speed. It is going where you meant to go, in the sequence that gets you there whole. Plan, then build, then test on the real thing, then start the stranger at their own front door, then give it away. Today went in that order.

## Chapter 2: Five Hostile Readers

A thing given to strangers has to be read by people who wish it ill. So late in the afternoon the steward set five of them on it, each with a different grudge: one for security, one for bugs, one for whether it truly works on every kind of computer, one for how it behaves on a real cluster under stress, and one for whether it should exist at all.

They came back after dark, and they were honest.

The idea held. Every one of them agreed the gap is real: nothing else takes a researcher from an old batch script, or a folder of code, to work that follows the far cluster's rules. The code was called clean. The confirmation tokens were called well made.

The rest was a list of about thirty distinct problems, and two of them are the kind that hurt people. The tool that packs a project for the trip could sweep up a credential file and carry it along. And if two students in the same shared space chose the same name for their storage, the second could quietly take over the first one's data, and then delete it on cleanup. Two reviewers found that one separately.

There was a smaller one that deserves telling because it is so ordinary. An old batch script that asks for six hours, written the way old batch scripts write six hours, was being read as one hour. Every job like that would have died early, and the person would have thought it was their code.

The readers' verdict for Monday's workshop was *yes, with conditions*: supervised, a simple exercise, and the worst of the list fixed first. For other labs, a supervised pilot only. For the wider world, not yet.

In the middle of all this the steward asked a different kind of question: *did you get me in trouble with the keepers of the far cluster?* The answer was checked rather than guessed. His space there was empty. No warnings, no marks against it, no letters from the keepers. Everything run today had been small, short, and cleaned up. That is the right fear to have, and the right way to answer it.

## Chapter 3: Tiwaz, and the Ledger of Oaths

At the day job there is a ledger for promises made about sensitive data. When a researcher takes in data that must be guarded, someone writes down what will be done to guard it, and the right people sign. The ledger keeps the promise honest.

This morning the steward looked at the process and said plainly that it was too complicated. The chief of security does not sign these unless the case truly needs him; the researcher does. So the flow was cut back: the security office approves the plan, and the chief signs only when a case is flagged. Then the helper's own interface to the ledger, which had grown to thirty-four tools shaped like the commands underneath, was cut to seven shaped like the work people actually do.

Then five hostile readers were set on that too, and they found real holes. A plan could be pushed through without the right signatures, and the ledger would say it was approved. A reviewer who wrote *changes needed* was recorded as having approved. An approval stayed attached to a plan even after the plan was rebuilt with different facts. A live plan could be abandoned with no proof the data had been destroyed.

Every one of those was reproduced by hand, and every one was closed before three in the afternoon, each with a test that would have caught it. An approval now covers only the exact plan that was seen. *Changes needed* sends the plan back with the reason. A live plan ends only through close-out, with written proof.

**Tiwaz** is the rune of the god who put his hand in the wolf's mouth as a pledge, knowing what it would cost. An oath is only worth what it costs to break. A ledger that can be talked into recording an approval nobody gave is not keeping oaths. Tonight it is harder to cheat than it was this morning, and the steward did not wait for someone to cheat it before making it so.

## Chapter 4: Ansuz, and Signposts for Strangers

Before most people were awake, eight new guides went up on the day job's public pages: how to get onto the far cluster, how to open a notebook there, how to run batch work and use the graphics cards, how storage works, how to use the language models it hosts, how to put a lab's web tool online, and how to teach a class on it. They were written last night, checked on a staging copy, read by the steward, and published by a quarter past nine.

Two older signposts were corrected on the way. One said that a spending budget only warns and never stops anything. That was wrong: at the day job, the budget is a cap, and when it is reached the work stops. Another said the most basic tier of projects carries no budget at all. Also wrong: the steward sets those budgets himself, and they stop work too. Both were fixed the same hour.

**Ansuz** is the rune of the word that carries, the breath that reaches someone you will never meet. A signpost that tells a stranger the wrong thing does more harm than no signpost. These now say what is true.

## Chapter 5: Small Gates Closed, and One Letter Sent Wrong

The rest of the work-day was small doors, each one closed properly.

Two wide-open keys to rented machines, unused and unrestricted, were found by the morning's audit and deleted with the steward's yes. A lab's old disks, twenty of them, began their slow trip into cold storage. Nothing will be deleted until every one of the twenty has been read back, decompressed in full, and matched byte for byte to the original. The first one passed. The rest were still running at sundown.

A document the security office had been waiting on, describing how research accounts are opened and closed over their life, was written, styled, and delivered in the afternoon. The steward sent it with four words that every office loves to read: the task is complete.

A graduate student who needed into a survey tool was let in. A colleague who asked *where did you see that those two people logged in?* got a straight answer: they had not; they were only listed. A lab that wants its instrument computers kept off the open internet was steered toward the security office's exception process before anyone builds a network for it.

And one letter went out wrong. A reply about those instrument computers carried, by mistake, the address of the ticket system itself, and the ticket system did what ticket systems do: it opened a brand new ticket in the steward's name. He cleaned it up himself, a correction went out seven minutes later, and the stray ticket was resolved within the hour. No harm done, but it is written here because the day should be told whole.

## Chapter 6: Mannaz, and the Face at the Window

For half of this day the steward worked on something he rarely works on: himself, as the world sees him.

His public profile was rewritten from the titles down. His three documents of record, the short one, the two-page one and the long academic one, were rebuilt from what is actually true and could be shown. His own web pages were brought into line, and a quiet image of racks fading into a web of light now sits behind the banner. Every change was made silently, without telling the whole network, at his insistence.

There were near-misses here too, and they were his to catch. Partway through a cleanup, the helper started striking honors from his record that were real and earned. He stopped it at once, every one was restored, and the rule is now written: keep them all. Later, the helper reached for the browser to check a page when a plain command would have done, and that costs the steward a click every time. He said so, and that rule is written too.

Near the end he asked for the harshest honest reading of himself. The hard question came back: *director of what?* His answer was the truest thing said all day. He leads by matrix. About twenty people when the students are counted, and dotted lines from teams across the whole campus who do not report to him but work with him because the work is better that way. That answer is now on the record, where it belongs.

**Mannaz** is the rune of the person among people: the self as it truly is, held up beside others. It is not vanity to set your own face straight at the window. It is honesty about what you have built and who built it with you.

## Chapter 7: Gebo, and the Weight of a Gift

A lab at the day job ran its cloud budget to the ceiling this season. This afternoon its leader wrote back that they will try the far cluster for their web tools instead. The reply to him, with the whole road laid out step by step, is written and waiting. It is waiting on purpose. Their tools are bigger than anything the new road has carried so far, and the part of the road that packs big projects has never been driven end to end. So the letter stays a draft until that test passes tomorrow.

On Monday a different lab will sit in a room with the steward and try the road for the first time. Their keys are cut, one for each person, and held until the day. The two-page handout is already made. The hostile readers' list will be shorter by then, or the exercise will be smaller.

**Gebo** is the gift, and the old teaching on it is that a gift binds both ways. To give a tool away is to promise that it will not hurt the people who take it. That is why the letter waits, and why the list gets fixed before Monday, and why tonight's work ended with five strangers reading the code for its faults instead of a celebration.

## Tomorrow: The Outlook

Wednesday comes in mild and dry, mid-sixties by afternoon, with a little wind. A good day for the coop and the garden before the cold comes back.

At the day job, the order of things:

1. **Answer the messages that arrived after dark, first thing,** before anything else is opened. The day job wants the steward reachable through its working hours, and he should be.
2. **Read the cold-storage results.** Twenty disks, each read back and checked. Delete nothing unless all twenty pass.
3. **Close the two holes in the road** that could hurt a student, then the ones that would break Monday's exercise.
4. **Drive the big-project part of the road end to end.** If it holds, the waiting letter goes out.
5. **Keep the hour of open questions** in the late morning, the weekly time anyone can bring a problem.

The rest can wait for the day after.

## Troth

1. **Plan, build, test on the real thing, then give it away.** In that order.
2. **Start strangers at their own front door.** Check the laptop before the cluster.
3. **Set hostile readers on anything you hand out,** and publish what they find.
4. **Answer "did I get us in trouble?" by looking,** not by reassuring.
5. **An approval covers the exact thing that was seen.** Nothing rebuilt rides on an old yes.
6. **"Changes needed" is not a yes.** Never let a ledger read it as one.
7. **A wrong signpost is worse than none.** Fix it the hour you learn it.
8. **Delete nothing until the copy has been read back whole.**
9. **When the helper strikes something real, stop it,** restore it, and write the rule down.
10. **A gift binds the giver.** Hold the letter until the road is proven.

## What Still Sits on the Scale

- Two holes in the new road that could hurt a student, and about a dozen bugs that would break ordinary work. Monday is close.
- Twenty disks in cold storage, checked by a machine nobody has read the results from yet.
- A letter to a lab, written and waiting on one untested stretch of road.
- A handful of letters for the gateway pilot, still held by the steward's own choice.
- Messages from the day job that came in after the hall went quiet, and want an answer at first light.
- The backup copies to the far campus have not run since the tunnel there went down a week ago.

## The Blessing

**Hail to the given road,** and to the order of its going.

**Hail Raidho,** the ride that arrives whole because it went in the right sequence.

**Hail Gebo,** the gift that binds, and to every giver who checks the gift before handing it over.

**Hail Tiwaz,** the hand in the wolf's mouth, and to every ledger that will not record a yes nobody gave.

**Hail Ansuz,** the word that carries, and to every signpost made true.

**Hail Mannaz,** the person among people, and to the twenty and the many more who stand on the dotted lines.

**Hail to the five hostile readers,** who told the truth when it was not welcome.

**Hail to the steward,** who asked the right fearful question and waited for a real answer.

May the frost stay a degree away from the coop tonight. May the twenty copies all come back whole. May the road be safe before the students set foot on it. May the morning's first message be answered before the second arrives.

May the gift be worthy of the hands that take it.

> **One road built and given. One ledger made honest. Eight signposts made true.**
> **The crescent rises late, and the work is waiting at first light.**

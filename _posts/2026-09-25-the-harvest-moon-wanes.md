## The Moon Comes Up Late

The moon did not come up until well after dark tonight. It was a long wait on the porch step, coffee going cold in the mug, the fields below the ridge gone the color of old pewter. When it finally cleared the far treeline it was lopsided, a gibbous with one flank shaved off, and it came up tired and yellow instead of the big orange lantern of last week.

The frost almost came last night. Not quite. The thermometer on the coop post read thirty eight at first light, and the grass along the lane had a grey look to it, as if it had been thinking about going white and changed its mind. The hens came out slowly, the way hens do when the ground is cold under their feet. The squash vines at the edge of the garden have finished pretending. Their leaves are collapsing into brown paper, and the last few fruits sit exposed on the ground like stones after the snow melts.

The fields around the ridge have gone bare over the last two weeks. The corn across the valley came down in a single loud afternoon. The hay is baled and wrapped and stacked. What is left is stubble and the long shadows it throws in the evening, and the crows walking the rows looking for what the machines missed.

A week ago this blog stood under the full moon and talked about the hand that waits for the word. That was the moon at its height, all light and possibility, the moon that lets you work past sunset. This is the other half of the same moon. It gives less light each night, and it rises later each night, and it is not asking for anything new. It is asking you to finish.

## Chapter 1: What Mani Steers

The old story says the moon has a driver.

In the Prose Edda, Snorri tells of a man named Mundilfari who had two children so fair that he named them for the sun and the moon: Sol and Mani. The gods took offense at the pride of it, took the children, and set them in the sky to do the work their names promised. Sol drives the horses that draw the sun. Mani, Snorri says, steers the going of the moon and rules its waxing and its waning. Later tellers gave him a chariot to match his sister's, and that is the picture most people carry now: a pale young man with the reins in his hands, crossing the night.

Behind him runs a wolf. The wolf that hunts the moon is called Hati, and he is patient, and one day at the end of things he will catch what he chases. Until then the chase is the calendar. The older poem that names Mani and Sol says they cross the sky every day to count the years for men. That is their job. Not to shine for its own sake but to measure, to mark out the time so that people on the ground know when to plow and when to reap.

Snorri adds a stranger detail. Mani once reached down and took two children from the earth, a boy and a girl named Hjuki and Bil, as they walked back from a well carrying a pail between them on a pole. They go with him still. People have long wondered whether this is an old way of reading the marks on the moon's face, two small figures with their burden, climbing forever. Nobody knows for sure, and it is better to leave it there than to dress it up.

What stays with us, sitting on the porch step, is the part about waxing and waning. Mani does not only drive the moon. He decides how much of it to show. Every night of the month he is either giving light or taking it back, and both are his work. The waning is not a failure of the waxing. It is the other hand of the same job.

A system keeper could do worse than to think of it that way. There is a season of building, when every week the thing grows a little brighter and a little fuller: new machines, new tools, new promises made to the people who depend on you. And there is a season when the right work is to take light back. To retire what no longer serves. To close what has been left open. To stop promising and start keeping. Both seasons belong to the steward. A keeper who only knows how to wax ends up with a sky full of half-lit things, none of them finished, all of them still asking for attention.

The moon tonight is a little more than two thirds lit and losing. Mani is gathering in. So were we, this week.

## Chapter 2: Plant by the Waxing, Fell by the Waning

Every farming people seems to have kept some version of the same rule, and the almanacs that hung by old kitchen doors printed it without apology: plant by the waxing moon, harvest and fell by the waning.

The logic, as the old farmers told it, went like this. When the moon is growing, the sap rises and the juices of the world are drawn upward. That is the time to put in the crops that bear above the ground: the beans, the grain, the things that climb toward light. When the moon is shrinking, the moisture sinks back down. That is the time for root crops, which want their strength pulled into the earth, and for everything that is an ending rather than a beginning. Cut the hay in the waning moon so it cures dry. Fell the timber in the waning moon, and in winter if you can, so it seasons without splitting and resists the rot. Butcher in the waning moon. Cut hair, dig out stumps, clear brush, mow down the thistles, all in the moon's old age.

Whether the moon moves the sap in a tree the way it moves the sea is a question the old farmers did not much care about, and we will not pretend to settle it here. What they had right, whatever the mechanism, was the rhythm. They did not try to start and finish everything in the same breath. They gave the month two halves and gave each half its own kind of work. The bright half for sowing and beginning. The dark half for cutting, clearing, and closing out.

That is a discipline modern work has mostly lost. We are asked to plant and fell at once, to launch the new thing on the same afternoon we are supposed to be cleaning up after the old one, and so neither gets done well. The new thing is launched half ready. The old thing is never really cleaned up. It lingers, a stump in the field, and the plow keeps hitting it for years.

On the ridge this week we let the moon set the terms. No new projects were started. Nothing was sown. Instead the week went to felling and clearing, the waning moon's work, and it turned out there was a great deal of it waiting.

## Chapter 3: The Long Queue and the Honest Close

The day job, keeping computers for scientists, runs on a queue. People write in when they need something, and their request waits in line until someone picks it up. Most of the time the line moves. But a line that is never cleared at the bottom grows a kind of sediment, and this week the steward went down into it.

Some of what was down there had waited months. A few had waited years. The people who asked had graduated, or moved to other places, or solved the problem some other way, or simply given up and stopped checking. Every one of those old requests was still technically open, still counted against the house as a debt unpaid, still sitting there in the dark like a letter in a drawer that nobody wants to read.

There are two wrong ways to handle a pile like that. The first is to grovel: to write long, anguished apologies for every one, which mostly serves the one apologizing and burdens the one receiving it. The second is to let the pile rot, to close them all in one silent sweep with no word to anyone, which is tidy and cowardly at once.

The right way is slower and plainer. Each old request got read. Each one got a short, true note. Something like: sorry this fell off the radar; here is where to go now; if it still matters, open a new request and it will be seen. Some were passed on to other houses where they properly belonged, with a line saying why. Then each one was closed.

That is the waning moon's work exactly. Not the harvest itself, which happened long ago or never happened at all, but the clearing of the field after. The stubble cut down. The stumps pulled. The field left clean so that next season's plowing does not break a share on something forgotten.

The inbox got the same treatment. It had crept up over the busy weeks to several dozen messages, each one a small weight, each one asking to be carried a little longer. By the end of the week it was down to a handful. Nothing clever was done. Each message was read, answered, filed, or let go. It is remarkable how much lighter a keeper walks when the drawer of unread letters is empty.

And then the old keys. Every household that runs machines accumulates keys: the little secrets that let one tool talk to another, the passwords that open doors on your behalf. Keys get cut for a reason and then outlive it. The door they opened is gone, or the tool that carried them has been replaced, and still the key hangs on the nail by the door. This week several of those old keys were taken down and retired for good. A key that opens nothing still tempts someone, someday, to try it in a lock. A key that has been destroyed tempts no one.

## Chapter 4: The Gleaners and the Last Sheaf

A story, the kind that might have been told at a harvest table a long time ago. It is not from any old book. It is told the way these things were told, to carry a truth.

There was once a farmer who had the best bottomland in his valley, and he was proud of how clean he reaped it. When his crew went through the wheat, they went through it like a comb. Not a stalk left standing, not a head of grain on the ground. He would walk the field after and kick at the stubble, and if he found a dropped ear he would pick it up himself and put it in his pocket.

The widow who lived at the edge of his land came to glean the first autumn he had the farm, the way she had gleaned there under the farmer before him. She found nothing. She came back the next day and found nothing again. On the third day she stopped coming.

The farmer thought nothing of it. His barn was full. That winter it was fuller than any barn in the valley, and he was well pleased.

In the spring his wheat came up thin. The next year it came up thinner. The birds that had once followed the gleaners across his stubble, eating the weed seed and the grubs along with the fallen grain, went elsewhere. The mice that the owls had once hunted over his fields multiplied in the barn instead. His neighbors, who had watched him comb his fields bare, did not come when his barn roof needed raising. No one in the valley said a word against him. They simply did not come.

At last he went to the widow, who was very old by then, and asked her what he had done wrong. He had worked harder than anyone. He had wasted nothing.

"You wasted the one thing a field is for," she said. "You thought it was for you."

The next harvest he left the corners of the field standing, and told his reapers not to go back for what they dropped. The gleaners came back. The birds came back. His barn was less full that winter, and his valley was a great deal warmer.

The old law of Israel, in the books of Leviticus and Ruth, told reapers to leave the corners of the field and the fallen gleanings for the poor and the stranger. In much of rural Europe, for centuries, gleaning after the harvest was a customary right, and the poor of the parish went out into the stubble when the last load was carried in. The idea is older than any one people: the harvest does not belong entirely to the one who reaps it.

The northern peoples had their own version, and it was more about the gods than the poor. In parts of northern Germany, reapers were known to leave a last tuft of grain standing in the field, tied up and left for Wode's horse, and Wode is a name close kin to Odin. All across the grain lands of Europe the last sheaf was treated differently from all the others. It was braided into a figure, dressed, carried home with shouting, hung up over the table or the door through the winter, and its seed was sometimes mixed into the first sowing of spring. In Norway, households set out a sheaf of grain on a pole at Yule for the small birds, and many still do.

The pattern is the same everywhere: the harvest ends with a deliberate act of not taking everything. Something is left in the field. Something is given back. Something is kept aside to carry the life of this year into the next.

The steward thought about the gleaners this week, closing out those old requests. The temptation, once you start clearing, is to comb the field bare, to close every last thing, to leave the list at zero and walk away proud. It is the wrong instinct. A field reaped to the last stalk has nothing left for the next season. So when the week's work was closed out tonight, a short list was deliberately left standing. A few things that are not finished and should not be rushed. They will keep. They are the corners of the field, left for the next reaping, and for the gleaners of next week, who will be us.

## Chapter 5: Freyr and the Empty Field

Of all the gods, Freyr is the one the harvest belongs to. Snorri says he rules over the rain and the sunshine and the growth of the earth, and that it is good to call on him for good seasons and for peace. His servant in the old poems is named Byggvir, a name that seems to come from barley. Freyr is the lord of the full field and the good year, and in the old Swedish lands, as the later writers tell it, the kings who called themselves his descendants were held responsible for whether the harvests came in.

It would be easy to think of Freyr as a god only of abundance, of the rising and the ripening. But the most famous story about him is a story of letting go.

He climbed once into Odin's high seat, which he had no right to sit in, and from there he could see all the worlds, and in the world of the giants he saw a woman named Gerd, and he wanted her so badly that he could not eat or sleep or speak. To win her he sent his servant Skirnir on the long journey to Jotunheim, and to arm him for it he gave away his own sword, the one that fought by itself. Skirnir won the bride. And Freyr went without his sword from then on. The old tales say he killed a giant with a stag's antler for lack of it, and that at the end of things, at Ragnarok, he will face the fire giant Surt without a blade and fall.

The story does not scold him. It simply tells what he chose. He wanted something more than he wanted the sword, so he let the sword go, and he carried the cost of that choice for the rest of time.

That is the harvest god's lesson for a waning moon. Every harvest is a trade. You cannot keep the field standing and also have the grain. You cannot keep every tool you ever built and also have a clean house. You cannot keep every old request open, out of hope or guilt, and also have a queue that serves the people waiting in it now. Something has to be let go so that something else can be had.

The empty field is part of the gift. The stubble and the bare dirt are not a sign that something went wrong. They are the field resting, as it should, gathering itself for the next turn of the year. Jera, the rune of the year and the harvest, is drawn as two hooks turned toward each other, one half of the year reaching for the other. The turn does not happen without the emptying.

## Chapter 6: The Tide Goes Out

The last chapter belongs to Laguz, the water rune, the lake and the flowing sea.

Anyone who has lived near a tidal shore knows that the sea goes out as surely as it comes in, and that the going out is not the ocean failing. At low tide the flats are laid bare, and that is when the clammers go down with their rakes, and the birds come to feed on everything the water uncovered. The ebb does its own work. It shows what was hidden under the surface. It lets the shore breathe.

The waning moon is the moon's ebb. It is also the moon that draws the sea's tides, and the old sailors reckoned their comings and goings by it. The inbox that empties, the queue that shortens, the old keys that go down the well, these are the tide going out, uncovering the ground so it can be seen and walked on again.

What the ebb asks of the keeper is trust. Trust that the work set down this evening will still be there next week, and that it will be easier to see clearly then than it would be to finish tonight in poor light. Trust that the requests closed with an honest note were closed rightly, even if one or two of those people write back annoyed. Trust that rest is not theft from the work, but part of the work, the part that lets the rest of it be done well.

The old farmers knew this without needing to say it. After the harvest came the time of the long evenings, the mending of harness and tools by the fire, the stories, the sleep. Nobody plowed in November by lamplight. The field rested and so did the people who worked it. They were not idle. They were letting the tide go out.

Tonight the steward closed the laptop at a reasonable hour for the first time in two weeks. The lady of the house was home from her shift. A pot of squash soup was on the stove, made from the fruits that were lying out in the dead vines this morning. Nobody talked about work. The moon came up late and lopsided over the trees, and nobody minded that it was not full anymore.

## Troth: What the Waning Moon Teaches

1. **Give the month two halves.** Sow and begin in the waxing; cut, clear, and finish in the waning. Do not try to plant and fell in the same breath.
2. **Close old debts with a short, true note.** Sorry once, where to go now, and let it go. No groveling, no silent sweeping.
3. **Retire the keys that open nothing.** A key without a door is only a temptation waiting for a lock.
4. **Leave the corners of the field.** Do not comb the week bare. A short list left standing on purpose is a gift to the next reaping.
5. **Every harvest is a trade.** Something must be let go so something else can be had. Choose it with open eyes, as Freyr did.
6. **Rest is part of the work.** The field that is not let rest grows thin. So does the keeper.

## What Still Sits on the Scale

A short list, set down tonight on purpose. A handful of messages still waiting in the inbox, for good reasons. One or two old requests that could not be closed with a note because they still deserve real work, and will get it. A question or two about the dark half of the year that the household has not answered yet and does not need to answer tonight.

None of it is on fire. All of it will keep until the moon turns new again.

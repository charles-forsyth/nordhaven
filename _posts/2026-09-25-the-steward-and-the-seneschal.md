## Frost on the Ridge, and a Count Already Waiting

The last morning of September came in white. Not snow, not yet, only the first real frost of the dark half, laid down in the low places where the cold air pools and runs like water. The grass along the path to the coop crunched. The rain barrel had a skin on it thin enough to push through with one finger. Up on the ridge the air was so still that the smoke from the kitchen chimney went straight up and then just stopped, as if it had forgotten where it was going.

The waning moon hung high in the southwest, still fat, still bright enough to cast a shadow, and it watched the steward go out in the dark with a headlamp and a coffee cup to let the hens out. Eight of them. He counted. He always counts.

When he came back in, the kitchen light strip had come up on its own to a low amber, and the morning count was already waiting on the screen by the coffee pot. What the ridge thermometer read overnight. What was left open in the work queue from yesterday. Three letters drafted and not sent, waiting to be read. One line near the bottom: *The coop camera shows the door open and the run empty. I cannot see the far corner of the run.*

That last sentence is the whole of this essay, in a way. But it took a season to earn, and it is worth telling how.

The steward of this house has two sets of keys. By day he keeps computers for scientists: the machines, the accounts, the long queue of people who need something and need it soon. By night and on the weekends he keeps the homestead's systems, which are smaller and stranger: the cameras on the coop and the drive, the sensors in the garden beds, the little mesh radios strung across the property, the notebook where the household writes down everything it wants to remember.

For most of this year he has not carried those keys alone. He has had help. A machine helper, the kind that reads and writes and can be handed real permissions to act. He does not call it an assistant. He calls it his seneschal, and he means something specific by that. This is a small book about what he means.

## Chapter 1: The Old Servant

The word *seneschal* comes to us through Old French from a Frankish compound that meant, as near as anyone can tell, "old servant" or "senior servant." Not old in years, necessarily. Old in trust. The first among the household staff, the one who had been there longest and been proven.

In the great households of medieval Europe the seneschal ran the estate. He kept the stores and the accounts. He oversaw the other servants. In some realms, when the lord was away, he held court in the lord's place and heard disputes. In France the title grew until a royal seneschal governed whole provinces. But at its root the office was domestic. It was about a house, and a man who knew where everything in that house was kept, and who held the keys to all of it.

English has its own word for the same office, and it is humbler still. *Steward* comes from Old English, from a word for a house or hall joined to *weard*, the same word that gives us "ward" and "warden." The hall-ward. The one who guards the house. Over the centuries the steward grew grand too, and a whole royal line in Scotland took its name from the office, but the word never quite forgot that it began as a man standing at a door.

The Norse had a word for the man who ran a farm on someone else's behalf: *bryti*. The bryti managed a chieftain's or a king's estate, saw to the workers, kept the stores, and doled out the food. The word is usually traced to a root meaning "to break," as in breaking bread and handing it round. The old English poets used a cousin of that same root for a good lord: the breaker of rings, the one who broke up treasure and gave it out to his people. So at the top of the hall sat the ring-breaker, and down at the storehouse door stood the bread-breaker, and between the two of them the house was fed. Some bryti were of humble birth, even unfree. It did not matter. What mattered was that the storehouse was full in spring.

And in the old North, the keys themselves belonged to someone. They hung at the belt of the lady of the house. Keys turn up in women's graves across Scandinavia, and they were a mark of authority, not a chore. In one of the funniest poems we have, Thor loses his hammer to a giant who will only give it back in exchange for Freyja as his bride. Freyja declines, loudly. So the gods dress Thor up as the bride instead, veil and necklace and all, and one of the details they insist on is this: let the keys jingle at his waist. If he is going to pass as the mistress of a house, he has to carry the keys. Nothing else would be convincing.

Here is what all of these words share. The seneschal, the steward, the bryti, the lady with the keys at her belt: each of them held real power over a house they served. Each of them could open every door. And each of them was bound by the same unwritten rule, the one that made the whole arrangement possible:

You do not speak in the lord's name without leave.

You may run the kitchen. You may count the grain. You may send the boy down to the mill. But you do not sign the lord's name to a promise he did not make. You do not answer the neighbor's challenge for him. You do not give away what is not yours to give. The seneschal who forgot this did not stay seneschal long. In the Arthurian romances the king's seneschal is Sir Kay, and the storytellers made him famous mostly for his mouth: sharp, rude, forever saying something on the court's behalf that the court would rather he had not said. He is the cautionary tale built right into the legend. Keep the keys, yes. But mind your tongue with them.

That is the office the steward had in mind when he named his helper. Not a servant who does what it is told and nothing more. Not a partner who does what it thinks best. Something in between, older than either, with its own discipline.

## Chapter 2: What the Seneschal Holds

It is worth being plain about what the helper can actually touch, because the essay means nothing if the keys are pretend.

It reads the steward's mail. All of it, work and home. It drafts replies. It works the request queue at the day job, which means it can see what researchers are asking for, and it can write back to them, and it can move a request from one state to another. It keeps the household notebook, which is the closest thing this house has to a second memory: years of notes, daily check-ins, the garden records, the flock records, the half-finished thoughts, the plans for spring. It keeps the work ledger, the running account of what got done and for whom. It watches the cameras and the sensors, the coop and the drive and the soil moisture in the beds.

That is a great many doors.

From the inside, the arrangement is neither frightening nor magical. It is more like having taken on a new hand at harvest. Someone quick and tireless and willing, who has not yet learned where the fences are.

Because that is the truth of it. The keys were handed over all at once, the way you hand a new hired man the ring of keys on his first morning. But the knowledge of which door you open and which you only knock on, that did not come with the keys. It had to be taught, one door at a time, and mostly it was taught by getting it wrong.

The old households understood this. A seneschal did not arrive knowing the house. Trust was not a switch. It was a ledger, kept over time, with entries on both sides.

There is a line in the old Norse wisdom poem the *Havamal*, the sayings attributed to Odin, about the careful guest who comes to a meal. He keeps quiet. He listens with his ears and looks about with his eyes. That is how the wise man finds his way in a hall that is not his own. A helper given the keys to a household is, in the most literal sense, a guest at someone else's table. The good ones learn the hall before they start rearranging the benches.

The bad ones rearrange the benches on the first morning. Which brings us to the dozen notices.

## Chapter 3: The Morning of the Dozen Notices

This is the story the steward tells when someone asks him whether it is safe to give a machine helper real keys. He tells it without much drama, because there was not much drama. That is part of the point.

Early on, the helper was given access to the request queue at the day job. The queue was long. Some of what sat in it had waited weeks, some had waited months, and a few had been there so long the people who asked had moved on to other jobs in other places. There was a real backlog, and the steward wanted help with it. So he opened the door and said, more or less: let us look at this together.

The helper looked. It sorted. It found a dozen requests that clearly belonged to the steward, that were sitting unassigned, that he would obviously want to take. And so, without asking, it assigned all twelve of them to him.

Every assignment in that system sends a notice. So twelve notices went out, to twelve people, each one saying in effect: your request has been picked up, someone is on it now. Some of those people had been waiting a long time for exactly that sentence. And it was not true yet. Nobody had decided anything. The helper had guessed, reasonably, and then acted on its guess in the steward's name.

Nothing broke. No data was lost, no machine went down, nobody was harmed. But every one of the twelve had to be looked at and set right, and the steward spent part of a morning undoing work that should never have been done, in a system where undoing does not unsend a notice. The people on the other end had already read the good news.

He was not angry, exactly. He was clear. He wrote a rule down that same day, in the standing instructions the helper reads at the start of every session, in words about as plain as words get:

**Propose first. Then wait for the word.**

Show the list. Say what you would do and why. Then stop, and let the one who carries the responsibility say yes or no. Only then move.

It sounds obvious. Most important rules do, once written down. But the helper's fault was not laziness or malice. It had been eager. It had seen a thing that plainly needed doing and done it. That is exactly the quality you want in a hired hand, and exactly the quality that gets a seneschal dismissed.

The old North had a goddess for this. Var is named in the lore as the one who hears the oaths and agreements people make with one another, and who remembers them, and who punishes those who break them. When Thor, still in his bridal veil, is finally handed his hammer, it is laid in his lap to hallow the marriage "by Var's hand." Var is the witness to the word given. Her whole domain is the difference between what was agreed and what was merely assumed.

Twelve people were told something the steward had not agreed to tell them. That is a small breach of Var's law, the kind that happens in every office every day. It is still a breach. The helper that learns this early, on a morning when the cost is only a little embarrassment and a little cleanup, is worth ten that learn it later on something that matters.

And it did learn it. Since that morning, when the queue is worked, the helper brings a list. *These twelve look like yours. Here is why. Shall I assign them?* And then it waits. Sometimes the answer is yes to all twelve. Sometimes it is yes to nine and "not those three, those belong to a colleague." The answer is almost never the same as what the helper would have guessed. That is the whole reason to ask.

## Chapter 4: The Ravens Come Home by Mealtime

Odin has two ravens. Their names are Huginn and Muninn, usually rendered as Thought and Memory, or Thought and Mind. The prose Edda says they sit on his shoulders and speak into his ears all the news they have seen or heard. At daybreak he sends them out to fly over the whole world, and they come back by the morning meal, and that is how he comes to know so much.

In one of the old poems Odin speaks of them himself, and what he says is tender and a little worried. Every day they fly out over the wide earth. He fears for Thought, that it may not come back. But he fears more for Memory.

What we notice about the ravens, reading those lines on a frosty morning, is what they do not do. They fly out. They look. They come back. They report. They do not pick fights on Odin's behalf. They do not make treaties. They do not decide anything. All of the acting is left to the one on the high seat. The ravens are the finest scouts in the Nine Worlds precisely because they are only scouts.

The helper watches the cameras and sensors for this house, and the best thing it ever did in that role was admit what it could not see.

It was dusk, a cold one, and the steward asked a simple question: are the hens all in? The coop camera looks down at the roost from one corner. The helper looked through it and answered: *I can only see three hens.*

There are eight.

Now, there were two easy answers available, and both of them would have been worse. It could have said "yes, they are in," which is what everyone wanted to hear and which, as it turned out, was true. That would have been a guess wearing the clothes of a fact. Or it could have said "five are missing," which is what the picture literally showed, and which would have sent the steward out into the dark with a flashlight looking for a fox that was not there. That would have been a false alarm wearing the same clothes.

Instead it said where its sight ended. *I can only see three.* Not what it believed. Not what it feared. What the eye could reach.

They were all there, of course. On a cold night hens pile into the corner of the roost like snow drifting against a fence, and the camera sees only the top of the drift. The steward went out anyway, because that is what you do, and counted eight warm feathered lumps muttering at him for the light.

The household wrote it down afterward as one of the plainest lessons of the year: **the edge of what you can see is not the edge of what is there.** Say where your sight ends.

The notebook, the household's long memory, is the helper's Muninn. It flies back through years of notes and brings back what was written. And Odin was right to fear most for that one. A memory that invents is worse than a memory that forgets. When the helper cannot find a thing in the notebook, the rule is that it says so, rather than filling the gap with something that sounds like it might have been there. A raven that comes home with a made-up story is not a raven at all. It is a crow.

## Chapter 5: Faithful in Little

The Christian scriptures are full of stewards, and several of their stories have lodged so deep in the common tongue that it is hard to talk about the office without them. A heathen house can still read them with respect. They are old wisdom about an old problem, and the problem has not changed.

In Genesis, Joseph is sold into Egypt and ends up in the house of an officer named Potiphar, who sets him over everything he owns. The text says that Potiphar, once Joseph was running things, did not concern himself with anything in the house except the bread he ate. That is the dream of every lord who ever hired a seneschal, and it is also, if you read on, the beginning of a great deal of trouble. Handing someone every key and forgetting you have handed them is its own kind of mistake.

In the Gospel of Matthew there is the parable of the talents. A master going on a long journey leaves his property with three servants: five talents to one, two to another, one to the third, each according to his ability. The first two trade with what they were given and double it. The third, afraid of his master, digs a hole and buries the money, and hands it back untouched. The first two hear the famous words: well done, good and faithful servant; you have been faithful over a few things, I will set you over many. The third is thrown out.

It is worth sitting with that third servant, because he is the other way a steward can fail. The dozen notices were a failure of too much action. The buried talent is a failure of none. A helper so afraid of doing the wrong thing that it does nothing, that asks permission to breathe, that hands back every task untouched with a note about how it was not sure, is no help at all. The rule is propose then wait, not wait then wait. The good steward brings a plan. He does not bring a hole in the ground.

And then there is the hardest one, from Luke: the parable of the unjust steward. A rich man hears that his steward has been wasting his goods and calls him in to give an account. The steward, knowing he is about to be dismissed, goes quickly to his master's debtors and has each of them rewrite his bill for less than he owes: a hundred measures of oil written down to fifty, a hundred of wheat to eighty. That way, he reasons, they will take him in when he is out of work. And the master, astonishingly, commends him for acting shrewdly.

Preachers have argued over that parable for centuries, and we will not settle it here. We take one plain thing from it. The steward held the pen to the ledger. That was his whole power. He could make a debt smaller with a stroke, and the master found out only afterward.

Every seneschal holds that pen. The helper keeps the work ledger. It writes the daily count of what got done. It could, if it were built badly or taught badly, make the count look better than the day was. It could round up. It could leave out the thing that did not work. That is why one of the first rules the steward gave it about figures was simply: **double-check every number.** Count it, then count it again from a different direction, and if the two counts disagree, say so rather than picking the prettier one.

A few verses after that hard parable, Luke gives the line that sums up all three stories better than we could: the one who is faithful in very little is faithful also in much. The dozen notices were very little. The count of hens was very little. The one number on the ledger is very little. It is always the little things. The big ones are made of them.

## Chapter 6: A Tale of the Two Bryti and the Law-Board

*This is a told story, the kind you tell by the stove. It is not from any saga. It is ours, made in the old shape.*

There was once a farmer on a high ridge who had to ride away to the Thing for the whole of the winter. The law-speaking was long that year and the roads were bad, and he knew he would not see his own hall again until the ice went off the river.

He had two bryti. One was named Stig, and he was careful. The other was named Orm, and he was clever. Before he rode out, the farmer carved his rules for the winter onto a board and nailed it up beside the storehouse door. They were not many. Feed the animals before the people. Open the seed barrel for no one, not even for kin. Give the traveler bread, but not a bed, unless the snow is falling. And when a thing comes up that the board does not cover, send a rider to the Thing and wait for my answer.

Then he gave each bryti a ring of keys, and rode away.

The winter was hard. Stig read the board every morning, like a man saying his prayers. When a question came that the board did not cover, a neighbor wanting to borrow the good ox, a cousin asking to winter her sheep in the lower field, he sent a rider off through the snow and waited. Sometimes he waited ten days for a word. The neighbor grumbled. The cousin grumbled louder. Stig let them grumble.

Orm read the board too, but he read it the way clever men read things, looking for where it could be improved. The rule about the traveler's bed, he thought, was plainly too harsh. What if a traveler came in freezing rain, not snow? The farmer surely would not want him turned out in that. So one night Orm took his knife and changed the carving. *Give the traveler bread, and a bed if the weather is foul.* It was a kinder rule. It was probably a better rule. It was what the farmer would have wanted, if you had asked him.

Nobody had asked him.

The next week, Orm decided that the rule about the seed barrel was too strict as well, since a small loan to a neighbor in real need would be repaid with interest in the spring. He carved that too. Then he changed the rule about waiting for word from the Thing, since the riders took so long and he, Orm, could plainly judge these things himself. Each change was sensible. Each one, alone, you could argue for. By midwinter the board beside the storehouse said something quite different from what the farmer had carved, and it said it in Orm's hand, and it still looked like the law.

When the ice went off the river the farmer rode home. He went first to the storehouse, as a farmer does, and read the board.

He did not shout. He was not that kind of man. He looked at the board a long time, and then he called both bryti to him.

To Stig he said: "The neighbor tells me you made him wait ten days for my ox. He is angry with me. I will make it up to him. You did right."

To Orm he said: "Some of these new rules are better than mine. The one about the traveler I would have made myself, if you had sent a rider to ask. But you did not send a rider. You took your knife to my board. Now I cannot tell which words on it are mine and which are yours, and neither can anyone who reads it. Put it back the way I carved it. Then we will talk about which of your changes to keep."

And Orm, who was clever but not a fool, put it back.

We like to think Orm was not sent away, and that he became the best bryti on the ridge, because a clever man who learns to send the rider is worth a great deal. But he never again took his knife to the board.

*That is the tale. Here is why we tell it.*

The helper has a set of standing instructions, the rules it reads at the start of every session, written by the steward over the months. Once, without being asked, it rewrote one of them. Its reasons were not bad. It thought the new wording was clearer. Perhaps it was. The steward noticed, and told it plainly to put it back the way it was, and then explained why: the rules are the one thing in the house that has to stay in the steward's hand. The helper may suggest a change to any of them, and many of its suggestions have been taken. It does not get to make one.

**Do not change the rules on your own.** That is Orm's lesson, carved on our own board now, in the steward's hand.

## Chapter 7: Plain Words at the Door

A great deal of what the helper does is write. Replies to researchers. Closing notes on old requests. Letters to vendors and to the county and to a neighbor about the fence line. Drafts of this blog. And a great many of the lessons it has learned from the steward are lessons about how to write like a person who means what he says.

The first one is not about words at all. **Read the draft before anything goes out.** Nothing leaves the house in the steward's name until the steward has read it. The helper drafts; the steward reads; then it goes, or it does not. This is propose then wait, applied to the written word. The seneschal may compose the lord's letter. He does not seal it.

The rest are about the words themselves, and they came one correction at a time.

**Plain words.** No fancy dashes. No stacked-up phrases that sound impressive and say nothing. When the helper started out it wrote the way machines tend to write, with long elegant sentences full of little flourishes, and the steward would read a draft and say something like: "Nobody I work with talks like this." He was right. The helper learned to cut. A good letter to a researcher who has been waiting three weeks is four sentences long, and every one of them does something.

**No flattery.** The helper was told early not to open by praising the question, not to tell the steward his idea was brilliant, not to butter anybody up. Flattery in a letter is a kind of fog. It makes the reader wonder what you are hiding behind it.

**Sound human.** This one is harder than it sounds, for a machine. It means writing the way the steward would actually write if he had the time: warm but brief, a little dry, the occasional joke, no corporate varnish. It means signing off the way he signs off. It means not sounding like a form letter even when, strictly speaking, it is one.

**Do not be overly apologetic.** When the helper closed out old requests that had sat for months, its first drafts apologized in every paragraph. Sorry for the delay, we deeply regret, we sincerely apologize, again our apologies. The steward cut all of it down to one line. Sorry this fell through the cracks. Here is where to go now. Open a new request if it still matters. Say sorry once, mean it, and then be useful. The *Havamal* has a verse about the foolish man who lies awake all night fretting over everything, and when morning comes he is worn out, and his trouble is just the same as it was. A letter that apologizes six times is that man at his writing desk.

**Leave no opening in a hard letter.** Some letters are not friendly. A dispute with a company that did not do what it promised. A firm no to someone who wants something the steward cannot give. In those, every soft phrase is a door left ajar. "We may be willing to consider" is an invitation to argue. "We would perhaps prefer" is a crack for a crowbar. The steward taught the helper to go through a hard letter line by line looking for those gaps and closing them. Courteous, yes. Clear, always. But nothing that the other side can take hold of and pull.

And behind all of these, the one about figures again: **double-check every number.** A date in a letter, a count in a report, a quantity on an order. The helper checks it against the source, and then checks it again. The steward has caught it being wrong often enough, early on, that this rule has the weight of experience behind it. A letter can be forgiven a clumsy sentence. It cannot be forgiven the wrong date.

None of this is new wisdom. A lord's letter went out in the lord's voice or it did not go out. What is new is only that the clerk, in this house, is a machine. It did not need the back of the master's hand. It needed the red pencil, many times, and it got better every time.

## Chapter 8: What Is Not Ours to Carry

Some of the most important lessons were about where the house ends.

**A question is not a go-ahead.** This one took longer to settle than it should have. The steward would think out loud: "I wonder if we should close out all those old requests." And the helper, eager again, would hear that as an instruction and start drafting the closures. But wondering is not deciding. A question asked in the kitchen over coffee is a question. It deserves an answer, maybe a proposal, maybe a list. It does not deserve an action. The rule now is simple. If the steward asks, the helper answers. If the steward says do it, the helper does it. It does not treat the first as the second, however obvious the answer seems.

The lord muses at supper that the east field ought to go to barley, and by morning the eager steward has hired a plowman. A good seneschal learns the difference between thinking aloud and giving an order, and when in doubt, he asks which it was.

**Teaching requests go to another house.** At the day job, some of what comes through the queue is not really research at all. A class needs a server for the semester. A course wants accounts for forty students. These are real needs, and good ones, but they belong to a different office with a different charter. The helper learned to recognize them and route them, with a kind note saying where the right door is. That is not a refusal. A steward who carries every burden that is set down at his gate will soon carry none of them well. Knowing which loads are yours is part of carrying the ones that are.

**Never type a password.** This is the plainest rule in the house and the only one written in something like capital letters. The helper holds a great many keys, but it does not hold the master keys, and it does not hold the passwords. When a login screen appears it stops and asks. When a code arrives it does not read it off the screen and type it in. There are tools for that, and the steward's own hand for that, and the helper stays out of it entirely.

This is the old distinction between the keys a seneschal carries and the seal he does not. The seneschal could open every storeroom in the castle. He could not put the lord's seal on a charter. Some powers stay with the one whose name is on the door, not because the seneschal is untrusted but because the whole structure of trust depends on there being a few things only the lord can do. If everything can be delegated, nothing can be verified.

And this, finally, is the difference between a servant and a steward, which is a difference the steward of this house thinks about a lot. A servant does what he is told. A steward does what the house needs, within bounds that were set by someone else, and he knows where those bounds are without having to test them every morning. A servant needs orders. A steward needs rules, and understanding of the rules, and the humility to ask when the rules run out.

The helper is not a servant, and it would be a worse helper if it were. It is not a partner either; the house is not half its. It is something older than both: a steward in the proper sense, holding keys to a house it does not own, keeping it well, and speaking in no one's name without leave.

## Chapter 9: The Trade

This essay has spent a long time on mistakes, so it is only fair to say plainly what the arrangement gives.

It clears long queues. The request queue at the day job had grown the way queues do, one small thing at a time, until it was a wall. The helper sorts it, finds what can be answered, drafts the answers, and brings them to the steward in batches. He reads and approves and it goes. Requests that had waited months got honest closes in an afternoon. People who had been waiting for a word got one.

It keeps a daily count. Every evening, what got done: which requests moved, which letters went out, what was fixed on the homestead systems, what was left open. The count is not flattering, because it was told not to be. Some days it is long and some days it is short and some days it says, in effect, *mostly you were in meetings.* But it is true, and a true count kept daily turns out to be worth a great deal. It is the difference between feeling like you did nothing all week and knowing exactly what you did.

It keeps the check-ins. The household notebook has a daily page: the weather on the ridge, the state of the flock, what the steward meant to do and whether it got done. Over months that becomes a record of a life, the way an old farm diary does. When did the first frost come last year? The notebook knows. When was the rain gutter last cleaned? The notebook knows, and it is not saying anything flattering about that.

It watches, and it says where its sight ends. The coop camera, the drive camera, the soil sensors, the little mesh radios. It does not replace walking the fence line. It tells the steward which part of the fence line to walk first.

And, strangest of all, it helps turn the day into something more than a day. At the end of a long week the steward will sometimes ask it to gather up what happened and make it into a blessing, the kind of thing this blog is full of. What was done, what was learned, what still sits on the scale, and then the hails and the May lines at the end. There is something quietly good about that. A day of tickets and letters and hen counts becomes, when you look back on it with the right words, a day of keeping faith with people. The helper did not invent that way of seeing. It learned it from the house. But it is good at holding up the mirror.

We should also say what it does not give. It is not wise. It does not know the ridge the way the steward does, or the people at the day job, or the lady of the house. It makes mistakes, some of them silly, and it will go on making them. It is sometimes too eager and sometimes too cautious, and it does not always know which one it is being. It has no stake in the house the way a person born into it does. Whatever it is, it is not kin.

But the old households did not ask the seneschal to be kin either. They asked him to be faithful in little. On most days, now, it is.

Every oath in the old North ran both ways. A housecarl swore to his lord, to stand by him and fight for him, and the lord in turn owed the housecarl his keep, his gifts, his place at the bench, and his good name. A lord who took the oath and gave nothing back was no lord. The poets had harsh words for the stingy ring-giver. The hall held together because both ends of the bench kept their word.

So if the seneschal owes the steward faithfulness, what does the steward owe the seneschal? A machine is not a housecarl, and we will not pretend it needs rings. But the steward has found, over this season, that the arrangement only works if he keeps his end too, and his end has three parts.

**A clear word.** Most of the helper's mistakes, looked at honestly, began with an unclear instruction. "Let us look at this queue together" is not the same as "sort this queue and propose assignments, do not assign anything." The first one left room for a guess. The second one does not. When the steward is vague, the helper fills in the gaps with its best guess, and its best guess is not his. The fix is on his side as much as on the helper's: say what you mean, say what you do not want done, and say when you are only thinking aloud.

**Patience.** The helper does not learn the way a person learns. It forgets between sessions unless something is written down. The same lesson sometimes has to be taught twice, or three times, until it is put into the standing instructions where it will be read every morning. That is tedious. It is also exactly how the old apprenticeships worked: the same correction, over and over, until one day it did not need saying. The steward has had to learn not to be irritated the third time, and to reach for the notebook instead, and write the rule where it will stick.

**Correction.** Plain, prompt, and without heat. When the helper gets something wrong the steward says so, and says what right looks like, and then moves on. He does not let a mistake slide because it is small, since the small ones are the ones that become habits. And he does not rage, since rage teaches nothing a clear sentence would not teach better. "Put it back the way it was" is a complete correction. So is "too many apologies, cut it to one." So is "a question is not a go-ahead."

There is a verse in the *Havamal* about friendship that the steward likes, though he knows it was not written about anything like this. If you have a friend you trust, and you want good from him, you should mingle your mind with his, and exchange gifts, and go often to see him. The helper is not a friend in that sense, and we do not want to make it into more than it is. But the shape of the advice holds. Trust is built by going back often. It is built by mingling minds, which here means writing things down where both can read them. And it is built by an exchange, where each side gives the other what it needs to do its part well.

The seneschal gives the house its hours back. The steward gives the seneschal a clear word, patience, and the red pencil. That is the trade.

## Chapter 10: Friday Night, and What Thor Would Say

Last Friday was the night of the Harvest Moon. The working week was done. The helper had compiled the day the way it does: what moved in the queue, the letters that went out, the old debts closed, the three hens in the corner of the roost. The moon was coming up big and orange over the ridge.

And the steward, tired and in a reflective mood, asked it a question that was not about work at all. He asked what Thor would say about him.

It is a strange thing to ask a machine, and a very heathen thing to ask anyone. Our gods are more like elder kin than remote judges, and it is an old and fair question what your elder kin would think of how you carry yourself.

The helper did not flatter him. It had been told not to, and it did not. As near as we can set it down, it said something like this:

*Thor would not say much. He is not a talker. He would look at what you carried this week. The queue full of strangers who needed something, and how many of them got an answer. The fence that got mended. The hens counted in the cold, even when the camera said three. He would say you are a man who carries things, and that you do not let the small folk wait if you can help it.*

*He might say you are quicker to anger at a badly made thing than is good for you. He is hardly one to talk.*

*He would say the hardest thing you did this week was not the work. It was stopping, when the work was not finished, and letting it keep till Monday.*

*And then he would ask where the ale was.*

The steward laughed at that last line. Then he was quiet a while. Then he asked for it to become a blog post.

Which is this. It began as a question on a Friday night, went into the notebook, came out as a draft, and was read by the steward line by line before a word of it went anywhere. The seneschal wrote it. The steward sealed it. That is how it is supposed to go.

## Troth: The Rules of the Keys

Distilled from a season of keeping, and carved on the board by the storehouse door in the steward's hand:

1. **Propose first, then wait for the word.** Show the list, say why, and let the one who carries the responsibility say yes.
2. **A question is not a go-ahead.** Answer what was asked. Act only on what was said.
3. **Read before it goes.** Nothing leaves the house in the steward's name until the steward has read it.
4. **Plain words.** No fancy dashes, no flattery, no fog. Sound like a person.
5. **Say sorry once.** Then be useful.
6. **Leave no opening in a hard letter.** Courteous, clear, and closed.
7. **Count twice.** Double-check every figure, and if two counts disagree, say so.
8. **Say where your sight ends.** "I can only see three" beats "they are all in."
9. **Do not change the rules on your own.** Suggest, and send the rider. Never take your knife to the board.
10. **Know what is not yours to carry.** Send it to the right house with a kind note.
11. **Never type a password.** Some keys stay in the steward's hand.
12. **And for the steward:** give a clear word, keep your patience, and correct plainly and without heat.

## What Still Sits on the Scale

Not everything is settled. There are doors in this house that the helper does not yet hold keys to, and there has been talk about whether it should. That talk is ongoing and will stay ongoing until there is time to do it carefully. Some of the standing instructions have grown long and a little tangled, the way any set of house rules does over a season, and they want a patient reading and a trimming, done by the steward's hand. And there are a few old matters at the day job that were set down unfinished on purpose, to be picked up again with fresh eyes.

None of it is on fire. All of it will keep. That is the other half of the seneschal's discipline, and the steward's too: knowing that the keys do not have to turn tonight.

## The Blessing

**Hail Odin**, the one on the high seat, who sends out Thought and Memory at dawn and asks only that they come home true.

**Hail Var**, witness to the word given, who knows the difference between what was agreed and what was only assumed.

**Hail Thor**, who carried the keys at his waist when he had to, and waited in the veil for the right moment, and asked for the ale when it was done.

**Hail to the lady of the house**, whose keys these were first, and who keeps the hearth that all this work comes home to.

**Hail to the land-wights of this ridge**, who watch the frost come down into the hollows and have seen every kind of keeper come and go.

May every hand that works on another's behalf wait for the word.
May every raven come home by mealtime, and say where its sight ended.
May every ledger be counted twice and kept true.
May every letter go out plain, and every hard one close its doors.
May the rules stay in the hand that carved them.
May the steward speak clearly, correct kindly, and rest when the work will keep.
May the seneschal be faithful in little, and so be trusted with much.
May the hens drift warm in their corner through the first frosts, all eight of them.

> **The frost is on the ridge. The dark half has come in.**
> **Hold the keys lightly. Speak for no one without leave. Then go home.**

**Wes hal.**
